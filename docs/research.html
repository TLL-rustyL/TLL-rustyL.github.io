Research interests

Light, molecules, and the spaces between.

We combine ultrafast spectroscopy, optical cavity engineering, and molecular design to discover how light reshapes molecular potentials and energy landscapes.

01

Polariton Chemistry and Lasing

Strong light–matter coupling creates hybrid quasiparticles known as polaritons, which combine molecular transitions (vibrational or electronic) with confined electromagnetic modes. Because polaritons possess both matter-like and light-like character, they provide a promising platform for controlling chemical reactivity, energy transport, and photonic behavior.

Despite rapid progress in this field, the molecular mechanisms underlying polariton-modified chemistry remain elusive, and general principles for designing polaritonic systems have yet to be established. At the same time, exciton-polariton systems offer considerable potential for miniaturized, low-threshold lasers and all-optical logic devices.

Our research pursues two complementary directions. First, we investigate how molecular interactions, energy transfer, and ultrafast dynamics vary under strong-coupling conditions. By connecting these dynamics with chemical outcomes, we aim to identify the molecular driving forces responsible for polariton-modified reactivity. Second, we combine molecular design with optical cavity engineering to promote exciton-polariton condensation and lasing. We also aim to develop tailored excitation pulse sequences that drive nonlinear parametric processes and enable all-optical switching and logic operations.

02

Self-Hybridized Polariton Systems

Conventional polariton experiments typically require an externally fabricated optical cavity, such as a Fabry–Pérot cavity or a metasurface. However, certain micro- and nanoscale droplets and single crystals naturally support high-quality-factor optical modes. When these modes are resonant with molecular transitions in the material, the two can hybridize to form light–matter states without a separately fabricated external cavity.

This geometry-enabled approach could simplify the construction of polaritonic materials while providing additional opportunities for chemical and materials design. For example, emitters (e.g., dye molecules) confined within metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) experience highly structured optical and chemical environments, which may facilitate strong light–matter coupling and enhanced lasing performance.

We seek to uncover how geometric confinement and self-supported optical modes give rise to unusual chemical reactivity and material behavior in micro- and nanoscale environments. We also explore chemical strategies for improving the tunability, functionality, and practical utility of polaritonic materials.

03

Instrumentation and Method Development

We develop and expand nonlinear vibrational spectroscopies to investigate photochemical processes across a broad range of timescales. These processes extend from local energy transfer, solvation, and molecular reorganization on femtosecond-to-picosecond timescales to chemical transformations occurring over microseconds to milliseconds.

By combining mechanical and digital delay methods, we aim to monitor chemical systems continuously across multiple stages of a reaction. Multidimensional spectroscopy provides a powerful way to resolve dynamic correlations, monitor changes in molecular interactions, and detect short-lived intermediates that may be hidden in conventional measurements.

Alongside instrument development, we develop analysis algorithms to extract kinetics spanning multiple orders of magnitude in time. We connect experimental observations with mechanistic models developed within our group and through collaborations with theoretical chemists, thereby constructing molecular-level descriptions of complex photochemical processes.
